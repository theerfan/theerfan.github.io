5. Information and conditional expectation

Rational expectations, Euler equations, and asset pricing all use the operator $\mathbb{E}_t$, the expectation given what is known at date $t$. Chapter 1 made information a σ-algebra. This chapter defines the expectation given a σ-algebra, shows that it is the best forecast available with that information, lists the rules for computing with it, and uses it to say precisely what an Euler equation asserts. The key shift is that a conditional expectation is not a number. It is a random variable: a forecast that depends on which state of the world you are in.

Throughout, $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ is a probability space, $X$ is an integrable random variable, and $\mathcal{G} \subseteq \mathcal{F}$ is a smaller σ-algebra, the information of some agent.

5.1 Conditioning on a partition

Start with information generated by a partition, as in the coin example. $\Omega$ is split into cells $G_1, \ldots, G_k$ with $P(G_j) \gt 0$, and $\mathcal{G}$ consists of all unions of cells. An agent with information $\mathcal{G}$ learns which cell the true state is in, and nothing more. Their best guess of $X$, having learned that the state is in $G_j$, is the average of $X$ over $G_j$:

$$ \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}](\omega) = \frac{\mathbb{E}[X\, \mathbf{1}_{G_j}]}{P(G_j)} \qquad \text{for } \omega \in G_j . $$

This is the elementary conditional expectation $\mathbb{E}[X \mid G_j]$, but assembled into a function of $\omega$. Before the cell is revealed, the forecast is unknown. It is a random variable, constant on each cell.

Two properties characterise this step function, and they are what generalise.

  1. It is $\mathcal{G}$-measurable: it is constant on each cell, so an agent with information $\mathcal{G}$ knows its value.
  2. It has the right averages: for every $G \in \mathcal{G}$, $\int_G \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\,dP = \int_G X\,dP$. On each cell this is how the average was defined, and every $G \in \mathcal{G}$ is a union of cells.

5.2 The general definition

Partitions are not enough. The information “today's productivity is $z_t$,” with $z_t$ normally distributed, splits $\Omega$ into uncountably many cells $\{z_t = z\}$, each of probability zero. The elementary formula divides by zero. The two properties above survive, and they become the definition.

Definition. A conditional expectation of $X$ given $\mathcal{G}$ is a random variable $Y$ such that

  1. $Y$ is $\mathcal{G}$-measurable, and
  2. $\int_G Y\,dP = \int_G X\,dP$ for every $G \in \mathcal{G}$.

It is written $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$.

Theorem. A conditional expectation exists, and any two are equal almost surely.

Why. Existence is the Radon–Nikodym theorem of Chapter 4. For $X \geq 0$, the formula $\nu(G) = \int_G X\,dP$ defines a measure on the smaller σ-algebra $\mathcal{G}$, and $\nu(G) = 0$ whenever $P(G) = 0$. So $\nu$ has a density with respect to $P$ restricted to $\mathcal{G}$, and that density is $\mathcal{G}$-measurable because it was produced on $\mathcal{G}$. Property 2 is the defining equation of the density. For general $X$, treat $X^+$ and $X^-$ separately. Uniqueness: if $Y$ and $Y'$ both qualify, then $G = \{Y \gt Y'\}$ is in $\mathcal{G}$, and $\int_G (Y - Y')\,dP = 0$ with a positive integrand, so $P(G) = 0$. The same argument applies to $\{Y \lt Y'\}$. ∎

“Equal almost surely” means a conditional expectation is a random variable defined up to null sets. Statements about it, such as an Euler equation, are statements that hold almost surely.

Conditioning on a random variable is conditioning on the information it carries: $\mathbb{E}[X \mid Z] = \mathbb{E}[X \mid \sigma(Z)]$. By the Doob–Dynkin lemma of Chapter 2, a $\sigma(Z)$-measurable variable is a function of $Z$, so

$$ \mathbb{E}[X \mid Z] = g(Z) $$

for some function $g$, and $\mathbb{E}[X \mid Z = z]$ is defined to mean $g(z)$. This is how expressions like $\mathbb{E}[v(a', z') \mid z]$ in a Bellman equation acquire meaning when $z$ is continuous. The function $g$ is unique only up to a set of $z$'s of probability zero under the distribution of $Z$. The value at one particular $z$ means nothing on its own. The function as a whole is what is defined.

5.3 The best forecast

When $\mathbb{E}[X^2] \lt \infty$, conditional expectation has a second description, which is the one economists use when they say “rational expectations.”

Theorem. Among all $\mathcal{G}$-measurable random variables $Y$ with $\mathbb{E}[Y^2] \lt \infty$, the conditional expectation minimises the mean squared forecast error:

$$ \mathbb{E}\bigl[(X - \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}])^2\bigr] \;\leq\; \mathbb{E}\bigl[(X - Y)^2\bigr]. $$

Moreover, the forecast error $e = X - \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ is uncorrelated with every such $Y$: $\mathbb{E}[e\,Y] = 0$.

Geometrically, random variables with finite second moment form a vector space with inner product $\langle X, Y \rangle = \mathbb{E}[XY]$. The $\mathcal{G}$-measurable ones form a subspace. Conditional expectation is the orthogonal projection onto that subspace, the closest point, and the error is perpendicular to the subspace. In words: the optimal forecast uses all the information, and what remains is unpredictable from that information. Any pattern left in the errors that could be detected from $\mathcal{G}$ would be a way to improve the forecast. This is the formal content of “agents do not make systematic mistakes.”

5.4 Rules for computing

All of the following hold almost surely, and each follows from the definition by checking properties 1 and 2.

  • Linearity. $\mathbb{E}[aX + bY \mid \mathcal{G}] = a\,\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] + b\,\mathbb{E}[Y \mid \mathcal{G}]$.
  • Taking out what is known. If $Z$ is $\mathcal{G}$-measurable, $\mathbb{E}[Z X \mid \mathcal{G}] = Z\, \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$. Known quantities act like constants. In particular $\mathbb{E}[Z \mid \mathcal{G}] = Z$.
  • Tower property (law of iterated expectations). If $\mathcal{H} \subseteq \mathcal{G}$, then $\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] \bigm| \mathcal{H}\bigr] = \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{H}]$. Your forecast of my forecast, when I know more than you, is your own forecast. The smaller information set wins.
  • Unconditional mean. $\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\bigr] = \mathbb{E}[X]$. This is the tower property with the trivial σ-algebra, or property 2 with $G = \Omega$.
  • Independence. If $X$ is independent of $\mathcal{G}$, then $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] = \mathbb{E}[X]$. Information unrelated to $X$ does not change the forecast. An i.i.d. shock tomorrow has the same conditional mean whatever happened today.
  • Monotonicity and Jensen. If $X \leq Y$ then $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] \leq \mathbb{E}[Y \mid \mathcal{G}]$, and for concave $u$, $\mathbb{E}[u(X) \mid \mathcal{G}] \leq u\bigl(\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\bigr)$.

The convergence theorems of Chapter 3 also hold conditionally, with the same hypotheses, so limits can be moved inside $\mathbb{E}[\cdot \mid \mathcal{G}]$ as before.

5.5 Filtrations and $\mathbb{E}_t$

Information over time is a filtration, an increasing sequence of σ-algebras

$$ \mathcal{F}_0 \subseteq \mathcal{F}_1 \subseteq \mathcal{F}_2 \subseteq \cdots \subseteq \mathcal{F}, $$

with $\mathcal{F}_t$ the information available at date $t$. Typically $\mathcal{F}_t = \sigma(z_0, z_1, \ldots, z_t)$, the history of shocks up to $t$. Then

$$ \mathbb{E}_t[X] := \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{F}_t]. $$

A sequence of random variables $(X_t)$ is adapted to the filtration if each $X_t$ is $\mathcal{F}_t$-measurable: its value is known at date $t$. Adaptedness is how a model says that decisions cannot use the future. When a planner chooses a consumption plan $(c_t)$ and the model requires it to be adapted, it is requiring that consumption at $t$ be a function of the history up to $t$, by the Doob–Dynkin lemma. Without that requirement a household could condition today's consumption on tomorrow's shock. Phrases such as “$c_t$ is chosen at $t$” or “a contingent plan” are informal versions of this one condition.

The tower property in this notation is $\mathbb{E}_t\bigl[\mathbb{E}_{t+1}[X]\bigr] = \mathbb{E}_t[X]$: today's forecast of tomorrow's forecast is today's forecast. Revisions of forecasts are therefore unpredictable, since $\mathbb{E}_t\bigl[\mathbb{E}_{t+1}[X] - \mathbb{E}_t[X]\bigr] = 0$.

A process $(M_t)$ is a martingale if it is adapted, each $M_t$ is integrable, and

$$ \mathbb{E}_t[M_{t+1}] = M_t . $$

The best forecast of tomorrow's value is today's. Every sequence of forecasts of a fixed quantity, $M_t = \mathbb{E}_t[X]$, is a martingale, by the tower property. Two martingales appear early in macroeconomics:

  • Hall's random walk. With quadratic utility, so that $u'$ is linear, and $\beta(1 + r) = 1$, the Euler equation becomes $\mathbb{E}_t[c_{t+1}] = c_t$. Consumption is a martingale, and changes in consumption are unpredictable from any information available at $t$. This is a testable restriction, and testing it started an empirical literature.
  • Discounted prices. With the risk-neutral measure $Q$ of Chapter 4 and a constant riskless rate, the price of an asset that pays no dividends, discounted at the riskless rate, is a martingale under $Q$.

5.6 Reading an Euler equation

A household chooses consumption and saves in an asset with gross return $1 + r_{t+1}$, known only at $t + 1$. Its Euler equation is

$$ u'(c_t) = \beta\, \mathbb{E}_t\bigl[(1 + r_{t+1})\, u'(c_{t+1})\bigr]. $$

With the tools of this chapter, every part has a precise meaning. The left side is an $\mathcal{F}_t$-measurable random variable. The right side is a conditional expectation, also $\mathcal{F}_t$-measurable and defined up to a null set. The equation asserts that the two random variables agree almost surely: in every history up to date $t$, outside a set of probability zero.

The definition of conditional expectation turns this into a family of unconditional statements. Write the Euler equation error as

$$ e_{t+1} = \beta (1 + r_{t+1})\, u'(c_{t+1}) - u'(c_t). $$

The Euler equation says $\mathbb{E}_t[e_{t+1}] = 0$. Multiplying by any $\mathcal{F}_t$-measurable $Z_t$ (with enough moments) and using “taking out what is known” and the unconditional mean rule gives

$$ \mathbb{E}\bigl[e_{t+1}\, Z_t\bigr] = 0 . $$

The Euler error is uncorrelated with every variable known at $t$: past consumption growth, past returns, today's interest rate, a constant. These orthogonality conditions are moment conditions, which is the basis for estimating preference parameters by the generalised method of moments, as Hansen and Singleton did with consumption data. The step from a conditional to an unconditional statement is property 2 of the definition, and it is the reason the definition was built that way.
