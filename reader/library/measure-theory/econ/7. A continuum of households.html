7. A continuum of households

This chapter assembles the previous six into the model they were chosen for: an economy with a continuum of households who face uninsurable idiosyncratic risk. It explains why macroeconomists use a continuum, how a population of labelled households becomes a distribution, what is subtle about the claim that idiosyncratic risk “averages out,” and how a stationary equilibrium is defined and computed. The chapter ends with an Aiyagari economy solved numerically, the source of the figures in Chapter 4, with each step tagged by the chapter that justifies it.

7.1 Why a continuum

Model the set of households as $I = [0, 1]$ with Lebesgue measure $\lambda$, so that each household $i$ is a point and a group of households $A$ has population share $\lambda(A)$ (Chapter 1). Two consequences make this convenient.

Every household is negligible. A single point has measure zero. Whatever household $i$ does, changing its behaviour alone changes no integral over households, so it changes no aggregate and no price. Price-taking is then an exact property of the model rather than an approximation for a large number of households. Aumann's 1964 paper “Markets with a continuum of traders” made this the standard formalisation of perfect competition.

Aggregates are integrals. With household $i$ holding assets $a_i$ and supplying effective labour $z_i$,

$$ K = \int_0^1 a_i \, di, \qquad L = \int_0^1 z_i \, di . $$

These are Lebesgue integrals over households (Chapter 3), and they require $i \mapsto a_i$ to be measurable (Chapter 2). With finitely many households they would be sums. The continuum keeps the averaging but removes the dependence on the number of households.

7.2 From labels to a distribution

The state of household $i$ is $s_i = (a_i, z_i)$ in the state space $S = [0, \infty) \times \{z_L, z_H\}$. The cross-sectional distribution is the pushforward of $\lambda$ by the map $i \mapsto s_i$ (Chapter 2):

$$ \Psi(B) = \lambda\bigl(\{i : s_i \in B\}\bigr), \qquad B \subseteq S \text{ Borel}. $$

By the change of variables theorem (Chapter 3), any aggregate of the form $\int_0^1 h(s_i)\,di$ equals $\int_S h\,d\Psi$. So

$$ K = \int a\, \Psi(da, dz), \qquad L = \int z\, \Psi(da, dz), \qquad C = \int c(a, z)\, \Psi(da, dz), $$

where $c(a, z)$ is the consumption policy. The labels $i$ have dropped out. The model's aggregate state is $\Psi$, a probability measure on $S$, possibly with atoms (Chapter 4).

Households move. If all of them follow the policy $a' = g(a, z)$ and draw $z'$ from the Markov chain $\Pi$, the natural guess for next period's distribution is

$$ \Psi' = \Psi Q_g, \qquad Q_g\bigl((a, z), A \times \{z'\}\bigr) = \mathbf{1}_A\bigl(g(a, z)\bigr)\, \Pi(z, z'), $$

the action of the policy-and-shock kernel on measures (Chapter 6). That guess is the subject of the next section.

7.3 Does idiosyncratic risk average out?

$\Psi Q_g$ is the probability distribution of one household's state tomorrow, if that household's state today is drawn from $\Psi$. Using it as the population distribution tomorrow requires that, among households with state $(a, z)$ today, the fraction who draw $z'$ is exactly $\Pi(z, z')$. Not approximately, and not on average over histories: exactly, in (almost) every realisation. That is a law of large numbers across households.

With finitely many households, the strong law of large numbers says it holds approximately, and exactly in the limit. If $N$ households draw independent shocks, the fraction who draw $z'$ converges to $\Pi(z, z')$ almost surely as $N \to \infty$. One expects the continuum to deliver the limit directly: with independent shocks $(\varepsilon_i)_{i \in [0,1]}$, the fraction $\lambda(\{i : \varepsilon_i = z'\})$ should equal the probability of $z'$.

It does not, at least not with the standard construction. Fix a history $\omega$ and look at the function $i \mapsto \varepsilon_i(\omega)$, which records every household's shock in that history. Because the shocks of any two households are independent, no matter how close their labels, this function jumps around at every scale like the most irregular noise imaginable. Judd (1985) and Feldman and Gilles (1985) showed what goes wrong. The joint map $(i, \omega) \mapsto \varepsilon_i(\omega)$ is not measurable with respect to the product σ-algebra on $[0,1] \times \Omega$ built from Lebesgue measure and $P$, except in trivial cases. So Fubini's theorem (Chapter 3) cannot be applied to it, and for a typical history the function $i \mapsto \varepsilon_i(\omega)$ need not be Lebesgue measurable. Then “the fraction of households that drew $z'$” is the measure of a set that may have no measure. The law of large numbers is not false. It cannot even be stated.

The problem has been resolved. Sun (2006) showed that one can enlarge the product σ-algebra, keeping the measures on households and on histories, so that Fubini's theorem still holds and independent processes indexed by a continuum exist on it. Such an enlargement is called a rich Fubini extension. On it, the exact law of large numbers holds: for almost every history, the cross-sectional distribution of the shocks equals their common probability distribution. The household space is then not $[0,1]$ with ordinary Lebesgue measure but an extension of it, which costs nothing in applications since no computation depends on the labels. Duffie and Sun extended the result to Markov chains with random matching, which covers the transitions of this model.

The practical conclusion is that $\Psi' = \Psi Q_g$ is a consistent modelling assumption, and every paper in this literature makes it, usually by writing “by a law of large numbers.” The same equation has a second reading that needs no law of large numbers at all: $\Psi$ is the probability distribution of a single household's state, at a date drawn far from the start. Numerically, this is what the code below computes.

7.4 A stationary equilibrium, assembled

The Aiyagari (1994) economy has three parts.

Households. Given an interest rate $r$ and a wage $w$, each household solves the Bellman equation of Section 6.3 with log utility, $\beta = 0.92$, earnings $z \in \{0.4, 1\}$ following the chain $\Pi$ of Figure 6.1, and the borrowing limit $a \geq 0$. The solution is a value function $v$ and a measurable policy $g(a, z)$ (Chapters 2 and 6).

Firms. A representative firm rents capital and labour and produces $Y = K^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}$ with $\alpha = 0.36$. Capital depreciates at rate $\delta = 0.08$. Competitive factor prices equal marginal products:

$$ r + \delta = \frac{\partial Y}{\partial K} = \alpha \Bigl(\frac{K}{L}\Bigr)^{\alpha - 1}, \qquad w = \frac{\partial Y}{\partial L} = (1 - \alpha) \Bigl(\frac{K}{L}\Bigr)^{\alpha} . $$

Markets. A stationary recursive competitive equilibrium is $(r, w, v, g, \Psi^*)$ such that

  1. $v$ and $g$ solve the household problem at prices $(r, w)$;
  2. $\Psi^*$ is invariant for the kernel $Q_g$: $\Psi^* = \Psi^* Q_g$ (Chapter 6);
  3. prices equal marginal products at $K = \int a\, d\Psi^*$ and $L = \int z\, d\Psi^*$ (Chapter 3).

Effective labour is fixed by the earnings chain: one third of households are in the low state (Section 6.4), so $L = \frac13 \cdot 0.4 + \frac23 \cdot 1 = 0.8$. Capital supply is not fixed. For each candidate $r$, the firm's condition gives $w$, the household problem gives $g$, the kernel gives $\Psi^*$, and integrating gives the capital households want to hold, $K^s(r) = \int a\, d\Psi^*_r$. Firms demand $K^d(r) = L\,\bigl(\alpha/(r + \delta)\bigr)^{1/(1 - \alpha)}$. Equilibrium is where they cross.

The equilibrium interest rate is 7.9%, below the 8.7% that would prevail with complete insurance. Households facing uninsurable risk save for precautionary reasons, the extra supply of capital pushes its return down, and capital is higher than under complete markets. That is Aiyagari's result, and every part of the calculation behind it is one of the objects defined in these notes.

The computation behind the figures (in .src/aiyagari.py) follows the definitions directly.

  1. Policy. Iterate on the Euler equation $u'(c) = \beta (1 + r)\, \sum_{z'} \Pi(z, z')\, u'(c')$ by the endogenous grid method until consumption converges. This is a fixed-point iteration of the kind justified in Section 6.3.
  2. Kernel. Store the distribution as masses on a grid of asset levels for each $z$. A choice $g(a, z)$ that falls between grid points $a_j \lt g \lt a_{j+1}$ sends mass to both neighbours, in proportions that preserve the mean. This is a transition kernel on the grid that approximates $Q_g$ in the weak sense of Section 6.5. The atom at $a = 0$ is a grid point, so it is represented exactly.
  3. Invariant distribution. Apply $\Psi \mapsto \Psi Q_g$ until it stops changing (Section 6.4).
  4. Aggregation. $K^s = \sum_{j, z} a_j\, \Psi(a_j, z)$, an integral against a discrete measure (Chapter 3).
  5. Equilibrium. Bisect on $r$ until $K^s(r) = K^d(r)$.

In the resulting equilibrium the capital–output ratio is 2.26, the wage is 1.014, and 5.8% of households hold no assets at all.

7.5 Aggregate shocks: distributions as state variables

Add an aggregate productivity shock $Z_t$, so output is $Y_t = Z_t K_t^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}$. Now prices move over time, and they depend on aggregate capital, which depends on the whole distribution $\Psi_t$. A household forecasting future prices must forecast the distribution, so its state includes $\Psi_t$:

$$ v(a, z; \Psi, Z) = \max_{a'} \Bigl\{ u(c) + \beta\, \mathbb{E}\bigl[v(a', z'; \Psi', Z') \bigm| z, Z\bigr] \Bigr\}, \qquad \Psi' = H(\Psi, Z). $$

The value function takes a probability measure as an argument, and the law of motion $H$ maps measures to measures. To make sense of continuity or approximation of functions of $\Psi$, the space of probability measures on $S$ needs a topology, and the standard one is weak convergence (Section 6.5). With it, the probability measures on a compact state space form a compact metric space, so “a continuous function of the distribution” has a meaning.

No computer can store a function of an infinite-dimensional object, and the literature has developed ways around it. Krusell and Smith (1998) replaced $\Psi$ by a few of its moments, typically aggregate capital $\int a\,d\Psi$, and found that households lose almost nothing by forecasting with the mean alone. Later methods perturb around the stationary equilibrium, either by linearising the law of motion of $\Psi$ in a finite approximation, or by working in sequence space with the response of aggregates to anticipated shocks (Auclert, Bardóczy, Rognlie and Straub, 2021). All of them are ways of computing with measure-valued state variables, and the objects they manipulate are the ones defined in these notes.

7.6 Where to go from here

The notes defined what was needed and stated the deeper results without proof: the Carathéodory and Kolmogorov extension theorems, Radon–Nikodym, existence of conditional expectations in full generality, the convergence theorems, and the theory of invariant measures. The general track of this library will prove them. For the macroeconomic side, Stokey, Lucas and Prescott, Recursive Methods in Economic Dynamics (1989), Chapters 7–13, covers measure theory, Markov processes, weak convergence and their use in dynamic models at the level of rigour these notes point toward. Ljungqvist and Sargent, Recursive Macroeconomic Theory, covers incomplete-markets models and their computation. For probability itself, Williams, Probability with Martingales, is a short, careful introduction that matches the order of topics here.
