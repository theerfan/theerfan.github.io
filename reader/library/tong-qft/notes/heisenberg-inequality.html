The Heisenberg inequality

Tong’s sentence is the estimate $\Delta p \geq \hbar/L$ for a particle trapped in a box of size $L$. That estimate is the sharp position–momentum inequality $\Delta x\,\Delta p \geq \hbar/2$, plus one fact about a wavefunction that fits inside the box: its position cannot fluctuate by more than $L/2$. Both steps are proved below. $\Delta$ means a root-mean-square deviation in a given state, not a resolution of a measuring device.

What is being bounded

Take a normalized state $\lvert\psi\rangle$, so $\langle\psi\vert\psi\rangle = 1$. For a Hermitian operator $A$ the expectation value and the variance in that state are

$$ \langle A\rangle = \langle\psi\vert A\vert\psi\rangle, \qquad (\Delta A)^2 = \big\langle (A - \langle A\rangle)^2 \big\rangle = \langle A^2\rangle - \langle A\rangle^2. $$

$\Delta A$ is the standard deviation of $A$. Write $\tilde A = A - \langle A\rangle$ for the deviation, and likewise $\tilde B$. Then $\tilde A$ and $\tilde B$ are Hermitian, $\langle\tilde A\rangle = \langle\tilde B\rangle = 0$, and $[\tilde A, \tilde B] = [A, B]$. The variances exist only when $\tilde A\lvert\psi\rangle$ and $\tilde B\lvert\psi\rangle$ lie in the Hilbert space, so the state has to be in the domain of the operators and of their squares. For position and momentum that is the requirement that $\int x^2\lvert\psi\rvert^2$ and $\int \lvert\psi'\rvert^2$ are finite.

Cauchy–Schwarz

On any inner-product space, two vectors $\lvert f\rangle$ and $\lvert g\rangle$ satisfy

$$ \lvert\langle f\vert g\rangle\rvert^2 \leq \langle f\vert f\rangle\,\langle g\vert g\rangle. $$

Apply this to the vectors produced by acting with the deviations,

$$ \lvert f\rangle = \tilde A\lvert\psi\rangle, \qquad \lvert g\rangle = \tilde B\lvert\psi\rangle. $$

Hermiticity gives $\langle f\vert g\rangle = \langle\psi\vert \tilde A\,\tilde B\vert\psi\rangle$, and the two norms are the variances:

$$ \lvert\langle \tilde A\,\tilde B\rangle\rvert^2 \leq (\Delta A)^2\,(\Delta B)^2. $$

The commutator is the part that cannot cancel

Split the product into its Hermitian and anti-Hermitian pieces,

$$ \tilde A\,\tilde B = \frac12\{\tilde A, \tilde B\} + \frac12[\tilde A, \tilde B], $$

where $\{X, Y\} = XY + YX$. The anticommutator of two Hermitian operators is Hermitian, so $\langle\{\tilde A, \tilde B\}\rangle$ is real. The commutator is anti-Hermitian, $[A, B]^\dagger = -[A, B]$, so $\langle[A, B]\rangle$ is purely imaginary. Real and imaginary parts add in quadrature:

$$ \lvert\langle \tilde A\,\tilde B\rangle\rvert^2 = \frac14\big\lvert\langle\{\tilde A, \tilde B\}\rangle\big\rvert^2 + \frac14\big\lvert\langle[A, B]\rangle\big\rvert^2. $$

The anticommutator term is non-negative. Dropping it only weakens the bound. Combined with Cauchy–Schwarz,

$$ (\Delta A)^2\,(\Delta B)^2 \geq \frac14\big\lvert\langle[A, B]\rangle\big\rvert^2, $$

which is Robertson’s form of the uncertainty relation:

$$ \Delta A\,\Delta B \geq \frac12\big\lvert\langle[A, B]\rangle\big\rvert. $$

Keeping the anticommutator instead of dropping it is the stronger Schrödinger relation. Nothing above used the form of $A$ and $B$, only that they are Hermitian and that the expectations exist.

Position and momentum

In the Schrödinger representation on wavefunctions of one coordinate,

$$ (X\psi)(x) = x\,\psi(x), \qquad (P\psi)(x) = -i\hbar\,\frac{d\psi}{dx}. $$

Acting on a wavefunction in the common domain,

$$ \begin{aligned} (XP\psi)(x) &= x\bigl(-i\hbar\,\psi'(x)\bigr), \\ (PX\psi)(x) &= -i\hbar\,\frac{d}{dx}\bigl(x\,\psi(x)\bigr) = -i\hbar\,\psi(x) - i\hbar\,x\,\psi'(x). \end{aligned} $$

Subtracting,

$$ \bigl([X, P]\psi\bigr)(x) = i\hbar\,\psi(x), $$

so $[X, P] = i\hbar$, as an operator identity on that domain, and $\langle[X, P]\rangle = i\hbar$ in every state where the two sides make sense. Robertson’s relation becomes Kennard’s inequality

$$ \Delta x\,\Delta p \geq \frac{\hbar}{2}. $$

The same calculation in three dimensions gives $[X_i, P_j] = i\hbar\,\delta_{ij}$, and therefore $\Delta x_i\,\Delta p_i \geq \hbar/2$ for each component separately. Components along different axes are not forced to constrain each other.

Equality holds when $\tilde X\lvert\psi\rangle$ and $\tilde P\lvert\psi\rangle$ are linearly dependent over $\mathbb{C}$ in the way Cauchy–Schwarz requires, and the anticommutator term vanishes. The states that do this are the Gaussians. A Gaussian has infinite tails, which matters for the box.

Why the box costs $\hbar/L$

Confine the particle to an interval of length $L$: the wavefunction vanishes outside it. Let $m$ be the midpoint of that interval. Every position the particle can be found at satisfies $(x - m)^2 \leq (L/2)^2$, so

$$ \big\langle (x - m)^2 \big\rangle \leq \Bigl(\frac{L}{2}\Bigr)^2. $$

The variance is the mean squared deviation about the mean, and the mean is the point that minimizes it. Deviating about $m$ instead can only be larger:

$$ (\Delta x)^2 = \big\langle (x - \langle x\rangle)^2 \big\rangle \leq \big\langle (x - m)^2 \big\rangle \leq \Bigl(\frac{L}{2}\Bigr)^2. $$

Hence $\Delta x \leq L/2$. Feed that into Kennard’s inequality:

$$ \Delta p \geq \frac{\hbar}{2\,\Delta x} \geq \frac{\hbar}{2\cdot(L/2)} = \frac{\hbar}{L}. $$

That is the inequality in the introduction. For a cubic box of side $L$ the same bound holds for each Cartesian component of momentum. If the particle is localized in a small region inside the box, $\Delta x$ is smaller than $L/2$ and the lower bound on $\Delta p$ is stronger than $\hbar/L$. The weakest the bound ever gets, among states that still fit in the box, is $\hbar/L$.

The two inequalities become equalities in different states. $\Delta x\,\Delta p = \hbar/2$ needs a Gaussian, which is not supported on a finite interval. $\Delta x = L/2$ needs the particle to sit at the two endpoints and nowhere in between, which is not a normalizable wavefunction in the domain of $P$. A state that is genuinely trapped in the box therefore has $\Delta p > \hbar/L$. Tong’s $\geq$ is the right direction.

The next clause in Tong

The sentence continues with $\Delta E \geq \hbar c/L$. That is not the Heisenberg inequality. It is a separate estimate: once $\Delta p$ is at least $\hbar/L$, the relativistic relation between energy and momentum gives an energy uncertainty of that order times $c$. The derivation above stops at $\Delta p$.
