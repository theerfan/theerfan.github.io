Hilbert space, and operator-valued fields

The recipe for turning a classical theory into a quantum one can be stated in a sentence: each classical coordinate becomes an operator on a Hilbert space. Applied to a field — which has one coordinate at every point of space — the recipe produces one operator at every point, an “operator-valued field”. The recipe is short, but each noun in it carries weight. This note builds the ideas up in order: what a Hilbert space is and why each part of its definition is needed; what an operator is and how it encodes a measurement; what it means to replace a classical coordinate by an operator; and how to picture a field whose values are operators rather than numbers, including the one sense in which that picture has to be refined.

The space the state lives in

A Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$ is a vector space over the complex numbers, equipped with an inner product, in which every Cauchy sequence converges. Each of the three parts has a physical reason.

Vector space. This is the superposition principle. If $|\psi\rangle$ and $|\phi\rangle$ are possible states, so is $\alpha|\psi\rangle + \beta|\phi\rangle$ for any complex $\alpha, \beta$. The coefficients are complex because interference requires relative phases.

Inner product. The inner product $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle$ is linear in the right slot, conjugate-linear in the left, and satisfies $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle \geq 0$, with equality only for the zero vector. It supplies probabilities (below), a length $\|\psi\| = \sqrt{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}$, and a notion of distance $\|\psi - \phi\|$.

Completeness. A sequence of vectors is Cauchy when its members get arbitrarily close to one another. Completeness says that whenever this happens, there is a vector in $\mathcal{H}$ that they approach. In finite dimensions this is automatic. In infinite dimensions it is not, and it matters because the objects physics computes with are limits: a wavepacket is the limit of a Fourier series, a position measurement with perfect resolution is a limit of finer and finer bins, and time evolution over a finite interval is the limit of a product of many short steps $(1 - iH\Delta t/\hbar)$. Without completeness the formalism would keep producing states that are not in the space.

Two examples cover most of ordinary quantum mechanics.

  • $\ell^2$, the square-summable sequences $(c_1, c_2, \ldots)$ with $\sum_n |c_n|^2 < \infty$, and inner product $\langle c|d\rangle = \sum_n c_n^* d_n$. Expanding a state in discrete modes (the energy levels of an oscillator, say) gives an element of this space; $c_n$ is the amplitude to be in mode $n$.
  • $L^2(\mathbb{R}^3)$, the square-integrable functions, $\int |\psi(\vec x)|^2\, d^3x < \infty$, where two functions that differ only on a set of zero volume count as the same vector. One spinless particle lives here. Strictly, $\psi(\vec x)$ is not the state but the components of the state in the position basis.

These two spaces are the same Hilbert space in different clothing. Any infinite-dimensional Hilbert space with a countable orthonormal basis is unitarily equivalent to $\ell^2$: just expand in the basis. What distinguishes the harmonic oscillator from the hydrogen atom is therefore not the Hilbert space. It is which operators are designated as position, momentum and energy, and what relations they obey.

Rays, bases, and what an operator is

The vector $|\psi\rangle$ contains more information than physics uses. Multiplying by an overall phase, $e^{i\alpha}|\psi\rangle$, changes no prediction. A physical state is a ray: the set of all nonzero multiples of a vector. Normalizing to $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle = 1$ fixes the magnitude and leaves the overall phase as a convention. If the system is in state $|\psi\rangle$, the probability of finding it in state $|\phi\rangle$ (both normalized) is $|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|^2$.

An orthonormal basis is a set $\{|n\rangle\}$ with $\langle n|m\rangle = \delta_{nm}$ such that every vector is a limit $\sum_n c_n|n\rangle$ with $c_n = \langle n|\psi\rangle$ and $\sum |c_n|^2 = \|\psi\|^2$. Changing basis does not change the state; it changes which set of questions the components answer directly.

An operator is a linear map $A$ that takes vectors to vectors. In infinite dimensions one has to be careful about where it is defined. An operator is bounded if $\|A\psi\| \leq C\|\psi\|$ for some constant $C$ and all $\psi$; bounded operators can be defined on the whole space. Most of the operators physics needs are unbounded. Position, $(X\psi)(x) = x\psi(x)$, can take a square-integrable function to one that is not, if $\psi$ decays too slowly. Momentum, $P = -i\hbar\,d/dx$, needs $\psi$ to be differentiable with square-integrable derivative. Such operators are defined on a dense subspace, their domain, which contains all physically reasonable states. This is not pedantry: the uncertainty relation $\Delta x\,\Delta p \geq \hbar/2$ only makes sense for states on which $X$, $P$, $X^2$ and $P^2$ are all defined.

Measurements are represented by self-adjoint operators: Hermitian ($\langle\phi|A\psi\rangle = \langle A\phi|\psi\rangle$) with a matching condition on domains. The reason is the spectral theorem, which is the precise version of “$A$ can be diagonalized with real eigenvalues”. When the spectrum is discrete, $A|n\rangle = a_n|n\rangle$ with real $a_n$ and orthonormal $|n\rangle$; a measurement of $A$ yields $a_n$ with probability $|\langle n|\psi\rangle|^2$, and these probabilities sum to one because the eigenvectors form a basis. When the spectrum is continuous, as for $X$, there are no normalizable eigenvectors: the “position eigenstate” $|x\rangle$ would be a delta function, which is not square-integrable. The symbol $|x\rangle$ is still useful in the resolution of the identity, $\int |x\rangle\langle x|\, dx = 1$, but it is an idealization, not a state that could be prepared. A wavepacket $\int dx\, f(x)|x\rangle$ with $f$ square-integrable is a state.

What “promote the coordinate to an operator” means

In classical mechanics a system with one degree of freedom is described by a point $(q, p)$ in phase space. An observable is a real function $f(q, p)$, and its value is simply $f$ evaluated at the point. Time evolution moves the point according to Hamilton’s equations, or, equivalently, changes each observable according to the Poisson bracket, $\dot f = \{f, H\}$, where $\{q, p\} = 1$.

Quantization keeps the list of observables and abandons the phase-space point. There is no state in which $q$ and $p$ both have definite values. Instead:

  • The state is a ray in $\mathcal{H}$.
  • The coordinate $q$ is replaced by a self-adjoint operator $Q$. The possible outcomes of measuring it are the points of its spectrum.
  • The momentum $p$ is replaced by an operator $P$, and the Poisson bracket is replaced by the commutator divided by $i\hbar$. For the canonical pair:
$$ \{q, p\} = 1 \qquad\text{becomes}\qquad [Q, P] = i\hbar. $$

Why a commutator? Both the Poisson bracket and the commutator are antisymmetric, bilinear, satisfy the Jacobi identity, and obey the product rule ($\{f, gh\} = \{f, g\}h + g\{f, h\}$, and likewise for commutators). The commutator is the natural bracket on operators with those properties; the factor $i$ makes $[Q,P]/(i\hbar)$ Hermitian, and $\hbar$ supplies the units of action. With this replacement the equation of motion carries over. In the Heisenberg picture, where operators carry the time dependence,

$$ \frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{1}{i\hbar}[A, H] = \frac{i}{\hbar}[H, A], $$

which is the classical $\dot f = \{f, H\}$ with the bracket replaced. Applied to $H = P^2/2m + V(Q)$ this gives $\dot Q = P/m$ and $\dot P = -V'(Q)$, Newton’s equations as operator equations.

A general classical function $f(q, p)$ should become an operator $F(Q, P)$, but this step is ambiguous whenever $q$ and $p$ are multiplied together, since $QP \neq PQ$. Various ordering prescriptions exist; none is forced. The ambiguity is of order $\hbar$ and disappears in the classical limit.

The expectation value $\langle\psi|F|\psi\rangle$ replaces “the value of $f$”. It is not a value the system has; it is the mean of the possible outcomes, weighted by the probabilities the state assigns. A state can have $\langle Q\rangle = 0$ while measurements of $Q$ almost never give zero. Each observable has its own probability distribution in a given state, and non-commuting observables do not have a joint probability distribution. That last fact is the content of $[Q, P] \neq 0$.

A field is a list of coordinates, one per point

A classical field at a fixed time assigns a number $\varphi(\vec x)$ to every point of space. Each value $\varphi(\vec x)$ is an independent coordinate, in exactly the sense that $q$ was, with its own conjugate momentum $\pi(\vec x)$. A field is therefore a system with infinitely many degrees of freedom. To see what the operators are, first make the infinity manageable by replacing space with a lattice of sites $\vec x_i$, each carrying one coordinate $q_i$ and momentum $p_i$, coupled to its neighbours.

For a single site the quantum theory is that of one degree of freedom, with Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}_i$. For the whole lattice the Hilbert space is the tensor product $\mathcal{H} = \bigotimes_i \mathcal{H}_i$: a basis state specifies the state of every site at once. The operator for site $i$ acts on this whole space, doing $Q$ on factor $i$ and nothing to the others,

$$ Q_i = I \otimes \cdots \otimes Q \otimes \cdots \otimes I. $$

Operators at different sites act on different factors and so commute, while each site has its own canonical pair. The relations are

$$ [Q_i, P_j] = i\hbar\, \delta_{ij}, \qquad [Q_i, Q_j] = [P_i, P_j] = 0. $$

Now let the lattice spacing $a$ go to zero. The site label $i$ becomes a continuous point $\vec x$, the coordinates become $\varphi(\vec x)$, and the momenta become a momentum density $\pi(\vec x)$, with $p_i \approx a^3\pi(\vec x_i)$. Since $\sum_i a^3 \to \int d^3x$, the Kronecker delta divided by the cell volume becomes a Dirac delta, $\delta_{ij}/a^3 \to \delta^{(3)}(\vec x - \vec y)$, and the relations become

$$ \bigl[\varphi(\vec x), \pi(\vec y)\bigr] = i\hbar\, \delta^{(3)}(\vec x - \vec y), \qquad \bigl[\varphi(\vec x), \varphi(\vec y)\bigr] = \bigl[\pi(\vec x), \pi(\vec y)\bigr] = 0. $$

This is what “operator-valued function” means. The input is a point $\vec x$. The output is an operator, and all of these operators act on one and the same Hilbert space — the space of states of the entire field — rather than on a separate space at each point.

In the Heisenberg picture the operators also carry time,

$$ \varphi(\vec x, t) = e^{iHt/\hbar}\, \varphi(\vec x, 0)\, e^{-iHt/\hbar}, $$

so a spacetime point goes in and an operator comes out. The state stays fixed; the operator used to ask a question at time $t$ differs from the one used at time $0$.

One caution about the earlier claim that all Hilbert spaces with a countable basis are equivalent. For finitely many degrees of freedom, a stronger statement holds: the relations $[Q_i, P_j] = i\hbar\delta_{ij}$ have essentially only one irreducible representation (the Stone–von Neumann theorem), so specifying the commutators specifies the quantum theory. For infinitely many degrees of freedom this uniqueness fails: the same commutation relations admit physically inequivalent representations. In practice one picks the representation built on the ground state of the Hamiltonian, as below.

The free field, concretely

For a free relativistic scalar field the Hamiltonian decouples into independent harmonic oscillators, one for each momentum $\vec p$, with frequency $E_{\vec p}/\hbar$ where $E_{\vec p} = \sqrt{\vec p^{\,2}c^2 + m^2c^4}$. Each oscillator has lowering and raising operators, and in units $\hbar = c = 1$ the field at a fixed time is

$$ \varphi(\vec x) = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\vec p}}}\Bigl(a_{\vec p}\, e^{i\vec p\cdot\vec x} + a_{\vec p}^\dagger\, e^{-i\vec p\cdot\vec x}\Bigr), $$

with $[a_{\vec p}, a_{\vec q}^\dagger] = (2\pi)^3\delta^{(3)}(\vec p - \vec q)$. The vacuum $|0\rangle$ is the state annihilated by every $a_{\vec p}$, and $a^\dagger_{\vec p}|0\rangle$ is a state with one quantum of momentum $\vec p$. Acting with the field on the vacuum,

$$ \varphi(\vec x)|0\rangle = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\vec p}}}\, e^{-i\vec p\cdot\vec x}\, a_{\vec p}^\dagger|0\rangle, $$

a superposition of one-particle states of every momentum, with phases that place the particle near $\vec x$. This is the concrete meaning of the field operator at a point: it creates (and, acting on other states, also annihilates) a quantum near that point.

A refinement: fields are operator-valued distributions

Compute the length of the state just written:

$$ \bigl\|\varphi(\vec x)|0\rangle\bigr\|^2 = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{2E_{\vec p}}, $$

which diverges, because at large $|\vec p|$ the integrand falls only like $1/|\vec p|$ while the measure grows like $|\vec p|^2$. So $\varphi(\vec x)$ at a single point does not take the vacuum to a vector in the Hilbert space. Strictly speaking it is not an operator. What is an operator is the field averaged against a smooth test function $f$ that falls off rapidly,

$$ \varphi(f) = \int d^3x\, f(\vec x)\, \varphi(\vec x). $$

With $\tilde f$ the Fourier transform of $f$, $\|\varphi(f)|0\rangle\|^2 = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{|\tilde f(\vec p)|^2}{2E_{\vec p}}$, which is finite because $\tilde f$ decays rapidly. The precise statement is that $\varphi$ is an operator-valued distribution: something that becomes an operator after being smeared over a region. This is the same refinement as “$|x\rangle$ is not a state, but a wavepacket is”. The intuition of an operator at each point survives; what is forbidden is asking for the field at one point with infinite resolution, which would require infinitely high momenta.

Where infinities come from

A single oscillator has zero-point energy $\tfrac12\hbar\omega$. A field is an oscillator for every mode, and there are infinitely many modes, so the free Hamiltonian contains a constant proportional to $\int d^3p\, \tfrac12 E_{\vec p}$ times the volume of space: infinite for two reasons, high momenta and infinite volume. Products of fields at the same point, which is what interaction terms are, run into the same short-distance problem in a stronger form: if $\varphi(\vec x)$ is not quite an operator, $\varphi(\vec x)^2$ is worse. Making sense of these products, and showing that the resulting infinities can be absorbed into the parameters of the theory, is the task of renormalization. The infinities are not mysterious. They are what one should expect from promoting a continuum of degrees of freedom to operators and then multiplying them at a single point.
