Pointlike electrons, extended protons

The electron and the proton have the same spin, $\tfrac12$, and charges of equal magnitude. Yet the electron is described as pointlike, while the proton has a size of a little under a femtometre ($1\,\mathrm{fm} = 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m}$). This note explains what “size” means for a quantum particle, how it is measured, what the measurements say about each particle, and why spin — despite the tempting picture of a spinning ball — has nothing to do with the difference.

How a size is measured: scattering and the form factor

Nobody can look at an electron or a proton directly. What can be done is to fire a probe at it and record how the probe is deflected. The size enters through interference.

Consider a fast charged probe scattering off a static charge distribution $\rho(\mathbf{r})$, normalized so that $\int\rho\,d^3r$ is the total charge. To first order in the interaction (the Born approximation), the scattering amplitude for a momentum transfer $\hbar\mathbf{q}$ is proportional to the Fourier transform of the potential. The potential of the distribution is the Coulomb potential of a point charge convolved with $\rho$, and the Fourier transform of a convolution is the product of the Fourier transforms. So the amplitude factorizes:

$$ \text{amplitude}(\mathbf{q}) = \text{amplitude}_{\text{point}}(\mathbf{q}) \times F(\mathbf{q}), \qquad F(\mathbf{q}) = \frac{\int \rho(\mathbf{r})\, e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\, d^3r}{\int \rho(\mathbf{r})\, d^3r}. $$

$F$ is the form factor. Physically, waves scattered from different parts of the charge arrive with different phases $e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}$, and $F$ is their average. A point charge has $F = 1$ at every momentum transfer. A distribution of size $R$ has $F \approx 1$ while $|\mathbf{q}| R \ll 1$, because all the phases are nearly equal, and $F$ falls once $|\mathbf{q}| R \gtrsim 1$, because the phases start to cancel.

The onset of the fall measures the size. Expand the exponential for small $q$:

$$ e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}} = 1 + i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r} - \tfrac12(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r})^2 + \cdots. $$

For a spherically symmetric distribution the linear term averages to zero, and the average of $(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r})^2 = q^2 r^2\cos^2\theta$ over directions is $q^2 r^2/3$. Hence

$$ F(q^2) = 1 - \frac{1}{6} q^2 \langle r^2 \rangle + \cdots, $$

where $\langle r^2\rangle$ is the mean-square radius of the charge. The slope of the form factor at $q^2 = 0$ is the radius. This is the precise sense of “pointlike”: a particle is pointlike, as far as anyone knows, if its form factor has not been seen to deviate from the point-particle prediction up to the largest momentum transfer probed. That is an experimental statement with an experimental limit, and it makes no reference to spin.

The resolution of such an experiment is set by the momentum transfer. To see structure of size $R$ one needs $q \gtrsim 1/R$, i.e. a probe momentum $\gtrsim \hbar/R$. Smaller structures require higher energies, which is why the search for substructure is pursued at the highest-energy colliders.

The proton

In the 1950s Robert Hofstadter scattered electrons of a few hundred MeV off protons and found that the form factor falls: the proton is extended. The charge radius extracted from the slope at $q^2 = 0$ is about $0.84\,\mathrm{fm}$. In the late 1960s, deep inelastic scattering experiments at SLAC went to much larger momentum transfers and found the opposite behaviour inside the proton: the scattering looked like scattering off pointlike constituents. These constituents, the partons, are the quarks and gluons.

In quantum chromodynamics the proton is a bound state of three valence quarks, together with gluons and a “sea” of quark–antiquark pairs. Its size is set by the scale at which the strong interaction becomes strong and confines quarks, $\Lambda_{\mathrm{QCD}}$, a few hundred MeV. Converting an energy to a length with $\hbar c \approx 197\,\mathrm{MeV\,fm}$,

$$ \frac{\hbar c}{\Lambda_{\mathrm{QCD}}} \sim 1\,\mathrm{fm}. $$

So the proton’s size is not a property of its spin or of its charge. It is the confinement scale of the force that binds its constituents. The electron does not participate in the strong interaction and has no such scale.

The electron

In the Standard Model the electron is an elementary field, with no spatial extent written into the theory. That does not mean its form factor is exactly $1$: quantum electrodynamics predicts small corrections, because the electron is constantly surrounded by virtual photons and electron–positron pairs. These corrections are calculable, and they depend on $q^2$ only logarithmically. An intrinsic size $R$ would instead add a correction of order $q^2R^2$, governed by a new scale.

No such correction has been seen. High-energy electron–positron collisions at LEP, analysed as a possible contact interaction from compositeness with strong coupling ($g^2/4\pi \sim 1$), bound the compositeness scale above roughly $10\,\mathrm{TeV}$. The corresponding distance is

$$ \frac{\hbar c}{10\,\mathrm{TeV}} \approx \frac{197\,\mathrm{MeV\,fm}}{10^{7}\,\mathrm{MeV}} \approx 2\times 10^{-20}\,\mathrm{m}, $$

tens of thousands of times smaller than the proton radius. The translation from a cross-section limit into a length depends on how the hypothetical contact interaction is normalized, so the number is a bound under a stated assumption, not a model-independent radius. Independently, the electron’s anomalous magnetic moment agrees with the QED prediction for a pointlike electron to about one part in $10^{12}$ of the moment; structure at anything like the proton’s scale would have spoiled that. None of this proves the electron has zero size. It says no size has been detected, down to distances far below a femtometre.

The spinning-ball worry

A natural objection: how can something with no size have angular momentum? If spin were literally rotation, a small enough ball would need its surface to move faster than light. This worry is worth working through, because the result applies equally to both particles — which is exactly why it cannot be what distinguishes them.

Give a uniform ball of mass $m$ and radius $r$ angular momentum $\hbar/2$. Its moment of inertia is $I = \tfrac25 mr^2$, so it spins at $\omega = (\hbar/2)/I$ and the equator moves at

$$ v = \omega r = \frac{5}{4}\,\frac{\hbar}{m r}, \qquad \frac{v}{c} = \frac{5}{4}\,\frac{\hbar}{mc\, r}. $$

Requiring $v < c$ forces $r > \tfrac54\,\hbar/(mc)$: the ball must be at least about as large as its Compton wavelength. Other mass distributions change the factor $\tfrac54$ but not the conclusion.

A second classical length comes from electrostatics. Assembling charge $e$ into a ball of radius $r$ costs energy of order $e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r)$. If the entire rest energy $mc^2$ were this electrostatic energy, the radius would be

$$ r_{\text{cl}} = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 m c^2} = \alpha\, \frac{\hbar}{mc}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 \hbar c} \approx \frac{1}{137}. $$

For the electron this “classical electron radius” is about $2.8\,\mathrm{fm}$. It is smaller than the Compton wavelength $\hbar/(m_e c) \approx 386\,\mathrm{fm}$ by the factor $\alpha$, so a ball of that radius carrying spin $\hbar/2$ would have $v/c = 5/(4\alpha) \approx 170$. The ratio $r_{\text{cl}}/(\hbar/mc) = \alpha$ holds for any particle whose mass is attributed to its Coulomb energy, so a proton treated the same way would have $r_{\text{cl}} \approx 2.8\,\mathrm{fm} \times m_e/m_p \approx 1.5\times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{fm}$ and the same absurd surface speed.

The real proton escapes this only because its mass is not electrostatic energy and its radius is not $r_{\text{cl}}$. At its measured radius of $0.84\,\mathrm{fm}$, with $\hbar/(m_p c) \approx 0.21\,\mathrm{fm}$, a uniform ball carrying $\hbar/2$ would have $v/c \approx \tfrac54 \times 0.21/0.84 \approx 0.3$, comfortably subluminal. But this is not evidence that the proton’s spin is a rotating ball either. Its spin is the total angular momentum — spins and orbital motion — of the quarks and gluons inside it.

The resolution of the worry is that spin is not rotation of a body. In relativistic quantum theory spin labels how a particle’s state transforms under rotations and boosts, i.e. which representation of the Lorentz group the particle’s field carries. A spin-$\tfrac12$ particle is described by a Dirac spinor field, and the Dirac equation coupled minimally to electromagnetism predicts a magnetic moment with $g = 2$ without any rotating charge distribution. The same description applies to the electron, the muon, and each quark. Spin is therefore silent on size. Scattering is what tells the two particles apart: the proton’s form factor falls at momentum transfers of order $\hbar/(1\,\mathrm{fm})$, and the electron’s has not been seen to fall at all.
