Why fermions and bosons differ

Every known particle with integer spin is a boson, and every known particle with half-integer spin is a fermion. This pairing between how a particle behaves under rotation and how many identical copies can share a state is the spin–statistics connection. It can be approached at two levels. In non-relativistic quantum mechanics the pairing is an experimental input: the theory is consistent either way, and one chooses the assignment that matches the world. In relativistic quantum field theory it becomes a theorem: with a stable vacuum and no influence outside the light cone, the opposite pairing is inconsistent. This note builds up both levels from the definitions, and then does the calculation that shows the wrong pairing failing.

Two separate definitions

Statistics is about exchange. Two particles of the same species are indistinguishable: no measurement can tell which is which. The state obtained by swapping them must therefore describe the same physics, so the wavefunction can change at most by a phase,

$$ \psi(\ldots, x_j, \ldots, x_i, \ldots) = e^{i\theta}\, \psi(\ldots, x_i, \ldots, x_j, \ldots). $$

Which phases are allowed is a question about topology. Think of an exchange as a path in which the two particles move around each other and end up in each other’s places. Doing the exchange twice brings each particle back to its start, and in three spatial dimensions the resulting closed path can always be continuously shrunk to “no motion at all” (the loop one particle makes around the other can be lifted over it). A continuous deformation cannot change a phase that takes discrete values, so a double exchange contributes $e^{2i\theta} = 1$, leaving $e^{i\theta} = \pm 1$.

The sign $+1$ is Bose statistics: the wavefunction is symmetric, and any number of particles may occupy the same single-particle state. The sign $-1$ is Fermi statistics: the wavefunction is antisymmetric, so if two particles are in the same state the wavefunction equals minus itself and vanishes. At most one fermion can occupy a given state. That is the Pauli exclusion principle.

Spin is about rotation of a single particle. It labels how the particle’s internal state transforms when the system is rotated. A rotation by $2\pi$ returns every vector to itself, and a particle of integer spin comes back to exactly the same state. For half-integer spin the state is a spinor, and a rotation by $2\pi$ multiplies it by $-1$; only a rotation by $4\pi$ is the identity. This is possible because quantum states are defined only up to phase, and the group of rotations has a double cover, $SU(2)$, whose element “rotation by $2\pi$” is $-1$.

Notice that neither definition mentions the other. Spin concerns one particle being rotated; statistics concerns two particles trading places. A priori, any combination might occur.

In quantum mechanics: an experimental input

The Schrödinger equation for $N$ identical particles is symmetric under exchange, so it preserves both the symmetric and the antisymmetric subspaces. Which subspace describes nature is an extra postulate, and nothing inside non-relativistic quantum mechanics forces it. A world of bosonic electrons with spin $\tfrac12$ would be a perfectly consistent non-relativistic theory.

Experiment decides. Photons, pions, $^4\mathrm{He}$ atoms and the Higgs boson have integer spin and are bosons; the blackbody spectrum, lasers, superfluid $^4\mathrm{He}$ and Bose–Einstein condensation are consequences of the symmetric counting of states. Electrons, protons, neutrons and neutrinos have half-integer spin and are fermions; the shell structure of atoms, the periodic table, the stability and incompressibility of ordinary matter, and the very different low-temperature behaviour of $^3\mathrm{He}$ (two protons, one neutron, two electrons: an odd number of fermions, so a fermion overall) are consequences of exclusion. The rule “integer spin, symmetric; half-integer spin, antisymmetric” is read off this catalogue.

In relativistic quantum theory: a theorem

The spin–statistics theorem (Fierz 1939, Pauli 1940, with general proofs in axiomatic field theory in the 1950s and 1960s) states that in any relativistic quantum field theory satisfying

  • Lorentz invariance,
  • a vacuum that is the state of lowest energy, with all energies bounded below,
  • a Hilbert space with positive norms, so that probabilities are positive, and
  • microcausality: observables at spacelike separation commute, so a measurement in one region cannot affect the statistics of a measurement in a causally disconnected region,

fields of integer spin must be quantized as bosons and fields of half-integer spin as fermions. Each wrong combination violates at least one of the assumptions.

There is a heuristic picture of why the two signs should match. An exchange of two particles can be continuously deformed, through the motion of the particles and their orientations, into a rotation of one of them by $2\pi$. (A physical model: tie the two particles together with a ribbon; swapping them and then untwisting leaves one particle rotated by a full turn. This is related to Dirac’s “belt trick”.) If the amplitude is to be insensitive to such continuous deformations, the phase acquired under exchange must equal the phase acquired under a $2\pi$ rotation: $+1$ for integer spin and $-1$ for half-integer spin. This picture captures the topology but is not a proof; in particular, it does not by itself show what goes wrong in a theory with the opposite assignment. The field-theory calculation below does.

The topology also explains why the number of dimensions matters. In two spatial dimensions a double exchange is not deformable to no motion (a loop in the plane around another particle cannot be lifted over it), so the exchange phase $e^{i\theta}$ can take any value. Particles with such intermediate statistics, anyons, exist as quasiparticles in the fractional quantum Hall effect. The sharp boson/fermion dichotomy is a feature of three spatial dimensions.

Watching the wrong choice fail: the Dirac field

Consider a free spin-$\tfrac12$ field $\psi(\vec x)$ obeying the Dirac equation, $i\partial_t\psi = h\,\psi$ with the one-particle Dirac Hamiltonian $h = -i\vec\alpha\cdot\nabla + \beta m$ (units $\hbar = c = 1$). The field’s energy is

$$ H = \int d^3x\; \psi^\dagger\, h\, \psi. $$

Here is the crucial structural fact. The Dirac equation is first order in time, and the Hamiltonian is linear in $h$. But $h$ has eigenvalues $\pm E_{\vec p}$, with $E_{\vec p} = \sqrt{\vec p^{\,2} + m^2}$, for every momentum. Expand $\psi$ in the eigenmodes of $h$: positive-energy spinors $u^s(\vec p)$ with coefficients $b^s_{\vec p}$, and negative-energy spinors with coefficients that we will call $d^s_{\vec p}$. With the modes orthonormalized, the energy becomes

$$ H = \sum_s\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\, E_{\vec p}\,\bigl(b^{s\dagger}_{\vec p} b^s_{\vec p} - d^{s\dagger}_{\vec p} d^s_{\vec p}\bigr). $$

Classically this is unbounded below: the negative-energy modes contribute $-E|d|^2$. (Contrast a scalar field, whose energy $\tfrac12\int(\pi^2 + (\nabla\phi)^2 + m^2\phi^2)$ is a sum of squares and positive by construction.) Quantization has to cure this, and the only freedom is the algebra imposed on $b$ and $d$.

The negative-energy coefficient $d$ must be reinterpreted. Physically, removing a negative-energy excitation should cost positive energy, so define $c^s_{\vec p}$ by $d^s_{\vec p} = c^{s\dagger}_{-\vec p}$ (the momentum label flips because removing momentum $\vec p$ is adding $-\vec p$): the operator that lowered the negative-energy mode now creates an antiparticle. Then

$$ H = \sum_s\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\, E_{\vec p}\,\bigl(b^{s\dagger}_{\vec p} b^s_{\vec p} - c^s_{\vec p}\, c^{s\dagger}_{\vec p}\bigr). $$

Now try each algebra, requiring that $c^\dagger$ create states of positive norm, i.e. that $c\,c^\dagger$ have a positive c-number part (for a single mode, $\langle 0|c\,c^\dagger|0\rangle = \|c^\dagger|0\rangle\|^2 > 0$).

Commutators. With $[c^r_{\vec p}, c^{s\dagger}_{\vec q}] = +(2\pi)^3\delta^{rs}\delta^{(3)}(\vec p - \vec q)$, we have $c\,c^\dagger = c^\dagger c + (\text{positive constant})$, so

$$ H = \sum_s\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\, E_{\vec p}\,\bigl(b^{s\dagger} b^s - c^{s\dagger} c^s\bigr) - (\text{constant}). $$

Each antiparticle created by $c^\dagger$ lowers the energy by $E_{\vec p}$. Adding more and more antiparticles lowers the energy without bound: there is no ground state. The only way to make the $c$-quanta carry positive energy with commutators is to take $[c, c^\dagger]$ negative, but then $\|c^\dagger|0\rangle\|^2 < 0$, a state of negative norm and negative probability. Either way, one of the assumptions fails. (Carrying out canonical quantization honestly, starting from the Dirac Lagrangian, one finds exactly this: imposing canonical commutators forces $[c, c^\dagger]$ to come out negative.)

Anticommutators. With $\{c^r_{\vec p}, c^{s\dagger}_{\vec q}\} = +(2\pi)^3\delta^{rs}\delta^{(3)}(\vec p - \vec q)$, we have $c\,c^\dagger = -c^\dagger c + (\text{positive constant})$, so the minus sign is flipped:

$$ H = \sum_s\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\, E_{\vec p}\,\bigl(b^{s\dagger} b^s + c^{s\dagger} c^s\bigr) + (\text{constant}). $$

Every particle and every antiparticle carries positive energy $E_{\vec p}$, the vacuum is the lowest state, and all norms are positive. As a by-product, $c^{s\dagger}_{\vec p}c^{s\dagger}_{\vec p} = 0$ (since $\{c^\dagger, c^\dagger\} = 0$), so no two quanta can occupy the same mode, and multi-particle states are antisymmetric under exchange. Fermi statistics is not inserted to match the periodic table; it is the unique choice that gives this spin-$\tfrac12$ field a stable vacuum and a positive Hilbert space.

Microcausality does not discriminate here. Observables such as the charge density are bilinear in $\psi$, and bilinears commute at spacelike separation whether the fields themselves commute or anticommute there. It is the energy that decides for the Dirac field.

The other half: the scalar field

For a free real scalar field of spin $0$ the roles reverse. Its mode expansion $\phi(x) = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\vec p}}}\bigl(a_{\vec p}e^{-ip\cdot x} + a^\dagger_{\vec p}e^{ip\cdot x}\bigr)$ has energy $\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\,\tfrac12 E_{\vec p}(a^\dagger_{\vec p}a_{\vec p} + a_{\vec p}a^\dagger_{\vec p})$, positive by construction, so energy does not single out an algebra. Causality does.

Define $D(x - y) = \langle 0|\phi(x)\phi(y)|0\rangle$, the amplitude for a quantum to propagate from $y$ to $x$. A direct computation gives

$$ D(x - y) = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\,\frac{1}{2E_{\vec p}}\,e^{-ip\cdot(x - y)}, $$

which is Lorentz invariant, and for spacelike separation $|\vec x - \vec y| = r$ it is nonzero, decaying like $e^{-mr}$. At spacelike separation a Lorentz transformation can reverse the time order of the two points, so $D(x - y) = D(y - x)$ there.

  • With commutators, $[\phi(x), \phi(y)] = D(x - y) - D(y - x) = 0$ at spacelike separation. The field, which is itself an observable, commutes with itself outside the light cone. Causality holds.
  • With anticommutators, $\{\phi(x), \phi(y)\} = D(x - y) + D(y - x) = 2D(x - y) \neq 0$ at spacelike separation. Since this is not zero, observables built from the field fail to commute outside the light cone, and a measurement here can influence one there. Causality fails. (The energy also collapses: $a\,a^\dagger + a^\dagger a$ becomes a constant, so $H$ is a number and the field has no dynamics.)

So for integer spin the consistent bracket is the commutator, and for half-integer spin it is the anticommutator. The general theorem extends these two examples to every spin.

Experiment or theorem?

Both, at different levels of description. With only the Schrödinger equation, the sign under exchange is an experimental fact that must be put in by hand. Once Lorentz invariance, a stable vacuum, positive probabilities and microcausality are required, the sign is forced by the spin, and the forced sign is the one experiments observe. Quantum field theory does not explain why spin-$\tfrac12$ particles exist. It explains why a spin-$\tfrac12$ particle cannot be a boson.
