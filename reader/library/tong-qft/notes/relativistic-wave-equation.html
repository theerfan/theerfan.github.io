What goes wrong with a relativistic one-particle wave equation

The Schrödinger equation $i\hbar\,\partial_t\psi = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi + V\psi$ has three properties that make it a sensible description of one particle. It is first order in time, so the wavefunction at one instant determines its future. Its free Hamiltonian $p^2/2m$ is bounded below, so there is a lowest-energy state and the particle cannot radiate away energy forever. And $\int|\psi|^2\,d^3x$ is conserved, so $|\psi|^2$ can be read as a probability density. It is not, however, compatible with special relativity: it is built on the non-relativistic energy $E = p^2/2m$.

The natural project is to keep the idea of a wavefunction for a single particle and simply replace $p^2/2m$ by the relativistic energy. This note follows that project through its three natural attempts and shows that each fails, in a different way: negative probabilities, an energy spectrum with no bottom, or propagation faster than light. Seeing exactly how each fails explains why the resolution is not a better wave equation but a different kind of theory.

Throughout the algebra, $\hbar = c = 1$; restoring them is a matter of dimensions. The relation to be turned into a wave equation is

$$ E^2 = \vec p^{\,2} + m^2. $$

Attempt one: the Klein–Gordon equation and negative probabilities

The Schrödinger equation arises from $E = p^2/2m$ by the substitutions $E \to i\partial_t$ and $\vec p \to -i\nabla$, which turn a plane wave $e^{-iEt + i\vec p\cdot\vec x}$ into an eigenfunction of both. Applying the same substitutions to $E^2 = p^2 + m^2$ gives an equation that is second order in time:

$$ \bigl(\partial_t^2 - \nabla^2 + m^2\bigr)\psi = 0. $$

This is the Klein–Gordon equation. It is manifestly Lorentz invariant, since $\partial_t^2 - \nabla^2$ is the invariant wave operator. But a second-order equation needs two pieces of initial data, $\psi$ and $\partial_t\psi$, rather than $\psi$ alone, which is already a hint that $|\psi|^2$ will not be the conserved quantity.

To find what is conserved, use the same trick that produces the probability current in Schrödinger theory. Multiply the equation by $\psi^*$, multiply the complex-conjugate equation by $\psi$, and subtract. The $m^2$ terms cancel, and what remains is a total derivative:

$$ \partial_t\bigl(\psi^*\partial_t\psi - \psi\,\partial_t\psi^*\bigr) - \nabla\cdot\bigl(\psi^*\nabla\psi - \psi\,\nabla\psi^*\bigr) = 0. $$

The bracket in the time derivative is purely imaginary, so multiplying by $i$ gives a real conserved density and current:

$$ \rho = i\bigl(\psi^*\partial_t\psi - \psi\,\partial_t\psi^*\bigr), \qquad \vec\jmath = -i\bigl(\psi^*\nabla\psi - \psi\,\nabla\psi^*\bigr), \qquad \partial_t\rho + \nabla\cdot\vec\jmath = 0. $$

Is $\rho$ a probability density? Test it on a plane wave $\psi = e^{-iEt + i\vec p\cdot\vec x}$, which solves the equation for $E = \pm\sqrt{p^2+m^2}$. Since $\partial_t\psi = -iE\psi$,

$$ \rho = i\bigl(-iE - iE\bigr)|\psi|^2 = 2E\,|\psi|^2. $$

For the negative-energy root, $\rho$ is negative. A negative number cannot be a probability. Superpositions make matters worse: because $\psi$ and $\partial_t\psi$ can be chosen independently at $t = 0$, $\rho$ can be made negative in any region one likes.

In the non-relativistic limit this density is harmless, which is why the problem is invisible at low energies. Write $\psi = e^{-imt}\varphi$ with $\varphi$ varying slowly compared with the rest frequency $m$. Then $\partial_t\psi \approx -im\psi$ and $\rho \approx 2m|\varphi|^2$, proportional to the Schrödinger probability density. The trouble comes from the extra solutions that the second-order equation admits — the negative-energy ones — for which the would-be probability has the wrong sign.

Attempt two: discard the negative root

The negative energies are a problem in their own right, quite apart from the density. The relation $E^2 = p^2 + m^2$ has two roots for every momentum,

$$ E = \pm\sqrt{\vec p^{\,2} + m^2}, $$

and the negative branch runs from $-m$ down to $-\infty$ as $|\vec p|$ grows. There is no lowest state. In a free theory this might be ignored, but couple the particle to electromagnetism, however weakly, and a particle in a negative-energy state can emit a photon and drop to a still more negative energy, then do it again, without limit. A theory whose particles can radiate an infinite amount of energy is unstable.

The obvious remedy is to keep only the positive root. Define a first-order equation with the positive square root as the Hamiltonian:

$$ i\partial_t\psi = \sqrt{-\nabla^2 + m^2}\;\psi. $$

The square root of an operator is defined through the Fourier transform: on a plane wave of momentum $\vec p$, $\sqrt{-\nabla^2+m^2}$ multiplies by $+\sqrt{p^2+m^2}$. The spectrum is then $[m, \infty)$, bounded below. The Hamiltonian is Hermitian on the usual inner product, so evolution is unitary and $\int|\psi|^2$ is a conserved, positive probability. Two of the three problems are gone.

The price is locality. $\sqrt{-\nabla^2+m^2}$ is not a differential operator: expanding it in powers of $\nabla^2/m^2$ gives an infinite series of ever-higher derivatives, and in position space it acts as an integral operator whose kernel spreads over a distance of order $1/m$. The rate of change of $\psi$ at one point depends on the values of $\psi$ elsewhere. The consequence of this is the third problem, examined below.

Attempt three: Dirac’s first-order equation

Dirac sought a Hamiltonian that is first order in derivatives — hence local — and whose square is the relativistic energy. Try a linear form,

$$ H = \vec\alpha\cdot\vec p + \beta m. $$

Squaring it gives $\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_j p_ip_j + m\sum_i(\alpha_i\beta + \beta\alpha_i)p_i + \beta^2m^2$. For this to equal $\vec p^{\,2} + m^2$ for every $\vec p$, the coefficients must satisfy

$$ \alpha_i\alpha_j + \alpha_j\alpha_i = 2\delta_{ij}, \qquad \alpha_i\beta + \beta\alpha_i = 0, \qquad \beta^2 = 1. $$

No numbers satisfy these, but $4\times 4$ matrices do, and the wavefunction becomes a four-component spinor. The equation is first order in time, and the density $\psi^\dagger\psi$ is positive and conserved. Two problems seem solved while keeping locality.

But the eigenvalues of $H$ are fixed by $H^2 = p^2 + m^2$: they are $\pm\sqrt{p^2+m^2}$. Taking the trace shows both signs occur equally: each $\alpha_i$ and $\beta$ is traceless (for instance $\beta = \beta\alpha_1^2$, and cycling $\alpha_1$ through the trace with the anticommutation relation gives $\mathrm{tr}\,\beta = -\mathrm{tr}\,\beta$), so $\mathrm{tr}\,H = 0$. The negative-energy tower has returned. Dirac’s response was to suppose that every negative-energy state is already filled, with the exclusion principle forbidding further electrons from falling in, and to interpret a vacancy in this “sea” as a positively charged particle, the positron. A sea of infinitely many electrons is not a one-particle theory. It is an early, awkward statement of the fact that the vacuum of a relativistic quantum theory is a many-particle state. The one-particle Dirac equation does not remove the tower; it relocates it.

The third problem: propagation outside the light cone

Return to the positive-root equation of attempt two, where energies and probabilities are both positive, and ask whether a particle initially confined to a region can be found outside the light cone of that region a moment later.

Suppose at $t = 0$ the wavefunction vanishes outside some ball. Its Fourier transform

$$ \tilde\psi(0, \vec p) = \int d^3x\, e^{-i\vec p\cdot\vec x}\,\psi(0, \vec x) $$

is then an entire analytic function of complex $\vec p$: giving $\vec p$ an imaginary part multiplies the integrand by a real exponential in $\vec x$, and the integral still converges because it runs over a bounded region. Conversely (the Paley–Wiener theorem), a function whose Fourier transform is entire and of bounded exponential growth vanishes outside a ball. Time evolution multiplies each Fourier mode by a phase,

$$ \tilde\psi(t, \vec p) = e^{-it\sqrt{\vec p^{\,2}+m^2}}\,\tilde\psi(0, \vec p). $$

The factor $e^{-it\sqrt{p^2+m^2}}$ is not entire: the square root has branch points where $\vec p^{\,2} + m^2 = 0$, at imaginary momenta of size $m$, and going around one flips its sign. For a generic initial wavefunction, multiplying by this factor produces a function that is not entire, so $\psi(t, \vec x)$ cannot vanish outside any ball. In particular it does not vanish outside the ball enlarged by $ct$, the region that the light cone permits. Some probability has appeared at spacelike separation from where the particle was. A detector there could fire, which is a signal faster than light.

This is not an artefact of the particular argument. Hegerfeldt showed that for any Hamiltonian bounded below, a state that is strictly localized at one instant immediately develops tails extending to infinity. Positivity of the energy alone forces the leak. So the very step that cured the negative-energy problem guarantees the causality problem.

The leak is small but not zero. The amplitude for the particle to travel from the origin to a point at distance $r$ in time $t$, at spacelike separation $r > t$, falls off as

$$ e^{-m\sqrt{r^2 - t^2}}. $$

Its range is $1/m$, the reduced Compton wavelength. Across a laboratory the effect is negligible; across a few Compton wavelengths it is not, and in principle causality is already violated because the amplitude is not exactly zero.

Taken together: keeping both roots gives negative probabilities or an unbounded-below spectrum; keeping only the positive root gives faster-than-light propagation. No choice of which root to keep disposes of all three problems at once.

What changes in quantum field theory

The way out is to stop asking a one-particle wavefunction to be the fundamental object. In quantum field theory the observables are built from fields, and the observables associated with a region of spacetime are built from fields in that region. Causality is then the requirement that observables in spacelike-separated regions commute, so that a measurement in one cannot change the statistics of a measurement in the other. For a free scalar field this can be checked directly. The commutator $[\phi(x), \phi(y)]$ equals the amplitude for a particle to propagate from $y$ to $x$ minus the amplitude to propagate from $x$ to $y$. Outside the light cone each of these is the nonzero tail just computed, but at spacelike separation there is a Lorentz transformation that exchanges which event comes first, so the two amplitudes are equal and the commutator vanishes.

In a theory with a conserved charge the second amplitude is naturally read as an antiparticle travelling the other way. The leak of a particle outside the light cone is cancelled, in every measurable quantity, by the corresponding antiparticle process. Pair creation is therefore not an optional extra added to a wave equation. It is half of the cancellation that makes the theory causal, and the reason that an equation for a fixed number of particles could not be causal, positive-probability, and stable all at once. The negative-frequency solutions, which were the problem in every attempt above, reappear in the field theory as positive-energy antiparticles.
