The de Broglie wavelength

A particle with momentum $\vec p$ behaves, in interference experiments, like a wave of wavelength

$$ \lambda_{dB} = \frac{h}{|\vec p|}. $$

This note derives that relation rather than postulating it: starting from light, where it can be read off from measured facts, and then showing that relativity forces the same relation on any particle once one accepts that a particle has an associated wave at all. It then asks what the wavelength is a measure of, and compares it with the other length a massive particle carries, the Compton wavelength $\hbar/(mc)$.

Light: the relation from two measured facts

A plane wave is described by its phase,

$$ \phi = \vec k\cdot\vec x - \omega t, $$

where the wavevector $\vec k$ points along the direction of propagation and $\omega$ is the angular frequency. The wavelength is the distance over which the phase advances by $2\pi$ at fixed time, so $\lambda = 2\pi/|\vec k|$.

For light two facts are known. The photoelectric effect and the blackbody spectrum show that light of frequency $\omega$ is exchanged in quanta of energy $E = \hbar\omega$. Electromagnetism says light travels at $c$, so $\omega = c|\vec k|$, and a pulse of electromagnetic radiation carrying energy $E$ carries momentum $|\vec p| = E/c$. Putting these together,

$$ |\vec p| = \frac{E}{c} = \frac{\hbar\omega}{c} = \hbar|\vec k|, $$

and so

$$ \lambda = \frac{2\pi}{|\vec k|} = \frac{2\pi\hbar}{|\vec p|} = \frac{h}{|\vec p|}. $$

Massive particles: relativity forces the same relation

de Broglie’s proposal (1924) was that matter, too, has an associated wave. Granting that, the question is which wave. Relativity answers it with no further assumption beyond $E = \hbar\omega$ in one frame.

The first step is to notice that the phase of a wave is a Lorentz scalar. The phase at an event counts how many crests have passed, and all observers agree on whether a given event sits on a crest or a trough. If $\phi = \vec k\cdot\vec x - \omega t$ is the same number in every frame for every event $(t, \vec x)$, then the coefficients $(\omega/c, \vec k)$ must transform exactly like the components of a 4-vector, because their contraction with the position 4-vector $(ct, \vec x)$ is invariant. So $(\omega/c, \vec k)$ is a 4-vector. The particle’s energy and momentum, $(E/c, \vec p)$, form another.

The second step is to compare them in one convenient frame. In the particle’s rest frame $\vec p = 0$ and $E = mc^2$. A wave describing a particle at rest has no preferred direction of travel, so it is a pure oscillation in time, $\vec k = 0$. Its frequency is fixed by applying the quantum rule $E = \hbar\omega$ to the rest energy:

$$ \hbar\omega_0 = mc^2, \qquad \vec k = 0. $$

In this frame, then,

$$ \left(\frac{\omega}{c}, \vec k\right) = \frac{1}{\hbar}\left(\frac{E}{c}, \vec p\right). $$

Both sides are 4-vectors. Two 4-vectors that are equal in one frame are equal in every frame, because they transform by the same linear map. Therefore, in any frame,

$$ E = \hbar\omega, \qquad \vec p = \hbar\vec k, $$

and the wavelength of a particle with momentum $\vec p$ is

$$ \lambda_{dB} = \frac{2\pi}{|\vec k|} = \frac{h}{|\vec p|}. $$

Two features follow at once. First, $\lambda_{dB}$ depends on the frame, because $\vec p$ does: in the rest frame it is infinite. By contrast the Compton wavelength $\hbar/(mc)$ involves only the rest mass and is the same for every observer. Second, the wave moves with the particle. A wavepacket travels at the group velocity, and using $\omega = E/\hbar$, $\vec k = \vec p/\hbar$ and $E^2 = p^2c^2 + m^2c^4$,

$$ v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk} = \frac{dE}{dp} = \frac{pc^2}{E} = v, $$

the velocity of the particle. (The phase velocity $\omega/k = E/p = c^2/v$ exceeds $c$, but the phase carries no signal.)

What the wavelength measures

$\lambda_{dB}$ is the distance over which the phase of the particle’s wave repeats. Wave behaviour — interference, diffraction, the failure of a sharp classical trajectory — becomes visible when a slit, an obstacle, or a difference between two path lengths is comparable to $\lambda_{dB}$. For a slow particle $|\vec p|$ is small and the wavelength is long. An electron with a kinetic energy of $50\,\mathrm{eV}$ has $p = \sqrt{2mE_k}$ and $\lambda_{dB} \approx 1.7\times 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}$, comparable to the spacing of atoms in a crystal, which is why Davisson and Germer saw electrons diffract off nickel. Nothing in the derivation says that $\lambda_{dB}$ is the size of the particle. It is a property of the particle’s state of motion.

Comparing with the Compton wavelength

A massive particle carries a second length, the reduced Compton wavelength $\lambda = \hbar/(mc)$. It marks the size of region below which confining the particle costs enough energy to create particle–antiparticle pairs. How do the two compare? Using $\lambda_{dB} = h/|\vec p| = 2\pi\hbar/|\vec p|$,

$$ \frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{dB}} = \frac{|\vec p|}{2\pi\, mc}. $$

So $\lambda < \lambda_{dB}$ exactly when $|\vec p| < 2\pi\, mc$. Writing $|\vec p| = \gamma m v$, the condition is $\gamma v/c < 2\pi$. (If both lengths are taken in reduced form, $\hbar/|\vec p|$ against $\hbar/(mc)$, the condition is $|\vec p| < mc$; the factor of $2\pi$ is a convention, not physics.) The Compton length is the shorter one for a particle at rest, for every non-relativistic particle, and for mildly relativistic ones. For a highly relativistic particle, $|\vec p| \gg mc$, the de Broglie wavelength is the shorter.

The highly relativistic case is precisely the one in which a single-particle wave stops being a complete description. A particle with $|\vec p| \gtrsim mc$ has energy $\gtrsim mc^2$, which is the scale at which pair creation becomes possible. The de Broglie picture — one particle, one wave, interfering with itself — is self-consistent only when pair creation is a small effect, and that requires $|\vec p|$ not large compared with $mc$. Throughout that regime $\lambda_{dB}$ is longer than the Compton wavelength: by a large factor for a slow particle, and by a factor of order one at the edge of the regime.

The edge is where the two ideas meet. Try to probe ever shorter distances with single-particle interference by raising $|\vec p|$. Just as $\lambda_{dB}$ shrinks towards a few times $\hbar/(mc)$, the particle’s energy reaches the pair threshold. The momentum needed for single-particle interference at sub-Compton distances is the same momentum that opens the door to creating new particles. Below the Compton wavelength, there is no clean one-particle diffraction experiment left to perform.
