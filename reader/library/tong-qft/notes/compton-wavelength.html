The Compton wavelength

Every particle of mass $m$ comes with a length,

$$ \lambda = \frac{\hbar}{mc}, $$

called the reduced Compton wavelength. It is built from the three ingredients that define the subject: $\hbar$ for quantum mechanics, $c$ for relativity, and $m$ for the particle. This note derives where the length comes from and what it means physically. The short answer is that $\lambda$ is the size of a region below which “one particle, located here” stops being a sensible description: squeezing a particle into a smaller region costs enough energy to create new particles. That is why relativistic quantum theory cannot be a theory of a fixed number of particles.

Two facts to combine

The argument uses one fact from quantum mechanics and one from relativity.

The quantum fact is the uncertainty principle. A state whose position is confined to a region of size $L$ has a momentum spread of at least

$$ \Delta p \gtrsim \frac{\hbar}{L}. $$

This follows from $\Delta x\,\Delta p \geq \hbar/2$ together with $\Delta x \leq L/2$ for anything confined to an interval of length $L$. The precise numerical factor will not matter below; what matters is that confining a particle forces its state to contain momenta of order $\hbar/L$, and the smaller the region, the larger those momenta.

The relativistic fact is the energy–momentum relation of a free particle of mass $m$,

$$ E(\vec p) = \sqrt{(pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2}, $$

which follows from $E/c$ and $\vec p$ being the components of a single 4-vector whose invariant length is $mc$. Unlike the non-relativistic $p^2/2m$, this relation contains a rest energy $mc^2$, and it says that energy can be traded for mass. That is the door through which new particles enter.

Where $\hbar/(mc)$ comes from

Take a particle, on average at rest, and confine it to a region of size $L$. Its state must contain momentum components of size $p \sim \hbar/L$, and the energy carried by such a component is

$$ E(L) = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\hbar c}{L}\right)^2 + (mc^2)^2}. $$

The energy that confinement costs, above the rest energy, is

$$ \delta E(L) = E(L) - mc^2. $$

There are two regimes, according to which term under the square root dominates. If the region is large, $\hbar/L \ll mc$, expand the square root:

$$ \delta E(L) \approx \frac{\hbar^2}{2m L^2} \ll mc^2. $$

This is the familiar non-relativistic confinement energy, the zero-point energy of a particle in a box. It is small compared with the rest energy, and the particle is slow. If the region is small, $\hbar/L \gg mc$, the momentum term dominates and the particle is ultra-relativistic:

$$ \delta E(L) \approx \frac{\hbar c}{L} \gg mc^2. $$

The changeover between the two happens where the terms under the square root are comparable,

$$ \frac{\hbar}{L} \sim mc \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad L \sim \frac{\hbar}{mc} = \lambda. $$

So the Compton wavelength is the size of region at which confinement energy becomes comparable to rest energy. Above it, confining a particle is a gentle, non-relativistic affair. Below it, confinement energy exceeds $mc^2$, which is enough energy to make more particles.

Why the threshold is a pair, and why it is $2mc^2$

Confinement does not by itself create anything. It forces the state to contain high-momentum components, and those components carry energy. Whether that energy can become new particles is decided by the conservation laws.

Suppose the particle carries a conserved charge: electric charge, or lepton number, or baryon number. The confined state must still have exactly that charge. Adding one more identical particle would change the charge, so it is forbidden. The cheapest addition that changes no conserved quantity is a particle together with its antiparticle, which carries the opposite charges. That pair costs at least $2mc^2$, one $mc^2$ each, when both are at rest.

Setting the confinement energy equal to this threshold, using the small-region estimate $\delta E \sim \hbar c/L$,

$$ \frac{\hbar c}{L} \sim 2mc^2 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad L \sim \frac{\hbar}{2mc} = \frac{\lambda}{2}. $$

Using the exact relation instead, and demanding that the total energy $E(L)$ reach the rest energy of the original particle plus a pair, $3mc^2$,

$$ \sqrt{\left(\frac{\hbar c}{L}\right)^2 + (mc^2)^2} = 3mc^2 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \frac{\hbar c}{L} = \sqrt{8}\,mc^2 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad L = \frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{8}\,mc} \approx 0.35\,\lambda. $$

The two estimates differ by a number of order one, as any estimate of this kind will. The physically meaningful statement is the scale: pair creation becomes possible when a particle is confined to a region of size of order $\hbar/(mc)$. Conventionally one drops the numerical factor and calls $\lambda = \hbar/(mc)$ the threshold.

Below this length, pairs are not a small correction

The claim that a region smaller than $\lambda$ is “full of pairs” is an order-of-magnitude statement about the structure of the state, and it can be unpacked as follows.

Suppose $L \gg \lambda$. Then the typical momentum $\hbar/L$ is much less than $mc$, the confinement energy $\hbar^2/(2mL^2)$ is far below $mc^2$, and there is not enough energy to make a real pair. Quantum mechanics still allows a pair to appear briefly as a virtual fluctuation, but the amplitude for that is suppressed: the energy mismatch is the full $2mc^2$, and only the far tail of a smooth wavepacket has momentum components large enough to help. The description “one particle in the box” is accurate, with small corrections.

Suppose instead $L \lesssim \lambda$. Now momenta of order $mc$ are not in the tail; a function confined to a region smaller than $\hbar/(mc)$ cannot be built from momenta much smaller than $mc$, so an order-one fraction of the state consists of such components. Each carries energy at or above the pair threshold. The state that was meant to be “a single particle, localized here” has an order-one overlap with states containing additional pairs. Finding extra particles in the region is then not a rare event but a typical one.

Shrink the region further, to $L = \lambda/N$ with $N \gg 1$. The confinement energy grows to $\delta E \sim N\,mc^2$, enough to pay for of order $N$ extra particles. The smaller the region, the more particle–antiparticle pairs the state can contain. These are not a classical gas orbiting the original particle. They are the additional particle content that any sufficiently localized state is forced to have, in any theory where energy can be converted into pairs.

The same conclusion appears from the point of view of measurement. To resolve structure on a scale $L$ a probe must transfer momentum of order $\hbar/L$. Below the Compton wavelength that momentum transfer is itself above the pair threshold, so the probe creates pairs in the very region it is trying to examine. No careful experimental design avoids this: it is a statement about what states exist, not about the quality of the apparatus.

The consequence for theory is direct. A wave equation for one particle, or for any fixed number of particles, cannot be a complete description at distances below $\lambda$, because the physics there changes the number of particles. A framework in which particle number is not fixed, where particles are excitations that can be created and destroyed, is required. That framework is quantum field theory.

Numbers

For the electron, $m_e c^2 \approx 0.511\,\mathrm{MeV}$, and with $\hbar c \approx 197\,\mathrm{MeV\,fm}$,

$$ \lambda_e = \frac{\hbar c}{m_e c^2} \approx \frac{197\,\mathrm{MeV\,fm}}{0.511\,\mathrm{MeV}} \approx 386\,\mathrm{fm} \approx 3.9\times 10^{-13}\,\mathrm{m}. $$

This is about a hundred times smaller than an atom, which is why atomic physics can be done with the one-particle Schrödinger equation. For the proton, $m_p c^2 \approx 938\,\mathrm{MeV}$ gives $\lambda_p \approx 0.21\,\mathrm{fm}$. In units where $\hbar = c = 1$ the Compton wavelength is just $1/m$: a mass and an inverse length are the same thing.

The factor of $2\pi$, and the origin of the name

The name comes from Compton’s 1923 experiment, in which X-rays scattered off electrons come out with a longer wavelength. The shift follows from energy–momentum conservation alone. Let a photon with 4-momentum $k$ hit an electron at rest, $p = (mc, \vec 0)$, and leave with $k'$ at angle $\theta$, while the electron recoils with $p'$. Conservation gives $p' = p + k - k'$. Squaring both sides, using $p^2 = p'^2 = m^2c^2$ and $k^2 = k'^2 = 0$,

$$ 0 = -2\,k\cdot k' + 2\,p\cdot(k - k'). $$

With photon energies $E$ and $E'$, $k\cdot k' = EE'(1 - \cos\theta)/c^2$ and $p\cdot(k - k') = m(E - E')$. Hence

$$ \frac{1}{E'} - \frac{1}{E} = \frac{1 - \cos\theta}{mc^2}. $$

Since a photon of energy $E$ has wavelength $hc/E$, multiplying by $hc$ gives the Compton formula,

$$ \lambda'_\gamma - \lambda_\gamma = \frac{h}{mc}\,(1 - \cos\theta). $$

The length that appears here is $h/(mc) = 2\pi\hbar/(mc)$, larger than $\lambda$ by $2\pi$. It is also the wavelength of a photon whose energy equals the rest energy $mc^2$. Both conventions are called the Compton wavelength; the reduced form $\hbar/(mc)$ is the natural one in relativistic quantum theory because it is the one that appears in wave equations, as in the factor $e^{-r/\lambda}$ governing how far a virtual particle of mass $m$ can reach. The pair argument fixes the scale, not the factor of $2\pi$.
