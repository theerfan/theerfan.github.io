Dirac 1931: a phase that acts as a field

A reading note on P. A. M. Dirac, “Quantised Singularities in the Electromagnetic Field,” Proc. R. Soc. Lond. A 133 (1931) 60–72, doi:10.1098/rspa.1931.0130. On page 65, between equations (5) and (6), Dirac says that if $\psi$ satisfies the usual wave equation for a free particle, then $\psi_1$ satisfies the wave equation for a particle of charge $-e$ moving in an electromagnetic field with potentials

$$ \vec{A} = \frac{\hbar c}{e}\,\vec\kappa, \qquad A_0 = -\frac{\hbar}{e}\,\kappa_0 . $$

This note fills in the steps behind that sentence. Dirac writes $h$ for what is now called $\hbar$; here it is $\hbar$ throughout, in Gaussian units.

Step 1: split off the phase

Dirac writes the wavefunction as

$$ \psi = \psi_1\, e^{i\beta}. $$

Here $\beta$ is a non-integrable phase. It has no definite value at a point, because going around a closed loop can change it. Its derivatives are well defined, though, and they get names:

$$ \vec\kappa = \nabla\beta, \qquad \kappa_0 = \frac{\partial \beta}{\partial t}. $$

Step 2: push the operators past the phase factor

This step is just the product rule. Apply the momentum operator $\vec p = -i\hbar\nabla$ to $\psi$:

$$ -i\hbar\nabla\left(e^{i\beta}\psi_1\right) = e^{i\beta}\left(-i\hbar\nabla\psi_1 + \hbar\vec\kappa\,\psi_1\right) = e^{i\beta}\left(\vec p + \hbar\vec\kappa\right)\psi_1 . $$

Do the same with the energy operator $W = i\hbar\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}$:

$$ i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(e^{i\beta}\psi_1\right) = e^{i\beta}\left(i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi_1}{\partial t} - \hbar\kappa_0\,\psi_1\right) = e^{i\beta}\left(W - \hbar\kappa_0\right)\psi_1 . $$

So acting on $\psi$ gives the same result as acting on $\psi_1$ with shifted operators, with an overall factor of $e^{i\beta}$ in front. The same holds when the operators are applied repeatedly, because the factor $e^{i\beta}$ comes out the front every time. For example,

$$ \vec p^{\,2}\psi = \vec p\cdot\left[e^{i\beta}(\vec p + \hbar\vec\kappa)\psi_1\right] = e^{i\beta}(\vec p + \hbar\vec\kappa)^2\psi_1 . $$

That is the sentence just before the passage: any wave equation $F(W, \vec p)\,\psi = 0$ becomes

$$ e^{i\beta}\,F\left(W - \hbar\kappa_0,\; \vec p + \hbar\vec\kappa\right)\psi_1 = 0, $$

and the factor $e^{i\beta}$, which is never zero, cancels.

Step 3: use the free-particle equation

Suppose $\psi$ obeys the free Schrödinger equation,

$$ \left(\frac{\vec p^{\,2}}{2m} - W\right)\psi = 0 . $$

By step 2, $\psi_1$ then obeys

$$ \left[\frac{(\vec p + \hbar\vec\kappa)^2}{2m} - (W - \hbar\kappa_0)\right]\psi_1 = 0 . $$

Nothing here depends on the Schrödinger equation in particular. The relativistic equations work the same way, since they are also built from $W$ and $\vec p$.

Step 4: recognise minimal coupling

In Gaussian units, a particle of charge $q$ in potentials $(A_0, \vec A)$ obeys the free equation with the substitutions

$$ \vec p \longrightarrow \vec p - \frac{q}{c}\vec A, \qquad W \longrightarrow W - qA_0 , $$

so that the Schrödinger equation reads

$$ \left[\frac{1}{2m}\left(\vec p - \frac{q}{c}\vec A\right)^2 + qA_0\right]\psi_1 = W\psi_1 . $$

With $q = -e$ the substitutions are $\vec p + \frac{e}{c}\vec A$ and $W + eA_0$. Compare these with step 3:

$$ \frac{e}{c}\vec A = \hbar\vec\kappa \;\Longrightarrow\; \vec A = \frac{\hbar c}{e}\,\vec\kappa, \qquad\qquad eA_0 = -\hbar\kappa_0 \;\Longrightarrow\; A_0 = -\frac{\hbar}{e}\,\kappa_0 . $$

These are exactly Dirac’s potentials. The charge $-e$ is a choice that makes the particle an electron. The argument only fixes the product of charge and potential, so any other charge would do with rescaled potentials.

Why this matters

The usual logic of gauge invariance runs from a field to a phase: given potentials, a gauge transformation changes the wavefunction by a phase factor. Dirac runs it backwards. He starts with a free particle carrying an arbitrary phase, and finds that the phase is indistinguishable from an electromagnetic field.

Whether that field is real depends on whether $\beta$ is integrable. The fields built from these potentials are

$$ \vec B = \nabla\times\vec A = \frac{\hbar c}{e}\,\nabla\times\vec\kappa, \qquad \vec E = -\nabla A_0 - \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial \vec A}{\partial t} = \frac{\hbar}{e}\left(\nabla\kappa_0 - \frac{\partial\vec\kappa}{\partial t}\right). $$
  • Integrable phase. If $\beta$ is an ordinary function, then $\vec\kappa = \nabla\beta$ has zero curl, and $\nabla\kappa_0 = \frac{\partial\vec\kappa}{\partial t}$ because derivatives commute. Both fields vanish. The potentials are a pure gauge transformation, the same thing as the shift by $\lambda$ in Section 1.1.
  • Non-integrable phase. Then $\nabla\times\vec\kappa \neq 0$ and $\vec B \neq 0$. By Stokes’ theorem, the change in phase around a closed loop $C$ bounding a surface $S$ is
$$ \Delta\beta = \oint_C \vec\kappa\cdot d\vec\ell = \int_S (\nabla\times\vec\kappa)\cdot d\vec S = \frac{e}{\hbar c}\,\Phi_B , $$

where $\Phi_B$ is the magnetic flux through $S$. This relation is what Dirac uses next. The wavefunction itself must be single-valued, so the phase change around any closed loop is a multiple of $2\pi$. Shrinking a loop around a point where the field could be singular then forces the flux out of that point to come in units of $2\pi\hbar c/e$. That is a magnetic monopole, and its strength $g$ must satisfy $eg = \frac{n}{2}\hbar c$ for an integer $n$.
